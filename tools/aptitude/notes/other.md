# Verbal Reasoning
## Number & letter series
Letter positions: A = 1 to Z = 26; memorise EJOTY = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. Opposites add to 27. Test gaps first, then alternate terms, then groups. **Trap:** a gap may itself form a pattern (+1, +2, +3).
## Coding-decoding
Compare word and code position by position. Common codes: shift, reverse, opposite letter, positions as numbers, or a sum. With word-to-word codes, find a word common to two sentences, then its code is the common code. **Example.** If CAT is 3120, the code is positions: 3-1-20.
## Blood relations
Draw a family tree: squares for men, circles for women, a double line for marriage and a vertical line for generations. Work from the last person mentioned. **Trap:** a name does not give gender, so only a clear word does ("son", "she"). Without one, the answer may be "cannot be determined".
## Direction sense
Draw it with north up. Right turn is clockwise. Shadows: at sunrise, your shadow falls west; at sunset, east; at noon, there's none to speak of. Use Pythagoras for the final distance. **Trap:** the question may ask for direction from the start or from the end, and they are opposites.
## Ranking & order
Total = rank from top + rank from bottom − 1. Between A and B = total − (A's position + B's position from opposite ends). When two people swap, use the change in rank to find the total. **Trap:** if positions overlap, people between are computed differently.
## Seating arrangement
Draw first. Place fixed facts, then definite relations, then eliminate. In a circle facing the centre, left is clockwise. Facing outwards, this reverses. In a row facing north, your left is the viewer's left.
## Puzzles
Build a grid with people as rows and attributes as columns. Mark ticks and crosses. Start from the most definite clue, and return to earlier clues after each new fact. When stuck, try the two remaining cases.
## Syllogisms
Draw Venn diagrams. Show every possibility. A conclusion is valid only if it is true in **all** of them. Two negatives or two "some" premises give no definite conclusion. "All A are B" also gives "some B are A". **Trap:** real-world truth doesn't matter, only the statements.
## Statement & conclusion
Use only what's stated. Reject conclusions with extreme words (all, never, only) unless the statement supports them. A conclusion must follow necessarily, not merely seem likely.
## Statement & assumption
An assumption is what the speaker takes for granted, without saying it. Test: if it were false, would the statement stop making sense? Reject anything stated outright or too extreme.
## Statement & argument
A strong argument is relevant, general and tied to the outcome. Weak ones are personal, trivial, emotional, or rest on an unproved assumption. Test each argument by itself, whether or not you agree with it.
## Course of action
A good course is practical, addresses the problem directly, is proportionate, and is within the authority of whoever acts. Reject extreme steps such as banning everything or closing an institution. Short-term relief can be valid when it fits.
## Cause & effect
Effect follows cause in time. Ask whether one can explain the other. If not, check for a common cause, or independence. A broad long-term state is often a cause and a specific event its effect.
## Analogy
State the relation in a sentence: "a doctor works in a hospital". Then apply exactly that sentence. Common types: part-whole, worker-tool, cause-effect, degree, function, and opposites. With numbers, test squares, cubes, n² ± 1 and multiples.
## Classification (odd one out)
Find a property that all but one share. If two properties fit, choose the more specific and meaningful one. For numbers, check prime, square, cube, multiple, and digit sum. For letters, check the gaps.
## Inequalities (reasoning)
Chain only signs in the same direction. A > B < C gives no relation between A and C. Any strict sign in a chain makes the result strict. "Either-or" answers occur when two conclusions together cover all cases, such as A ≥ B or A < B.
## Input-output
Compare consecutive steps to find one rule: words sorted, numbers moved, or an arithmetic change. Note how many items are fixed per step. Then jump to the step you need instead of doing all.
## Mathematical operations
Replace symbols first, then apply BODMAS. To check an equation, try the given interchange on each option. **Trap:** BODMAS applies after replacement, not to the symbols as first written.
## Alphanumeric series
Mark the counting direction. "Xth to the left of the Yth from the right" means add positions when moving away from that end. For conditions such as "digit before a letter", check each triple.
## Truth & lie puzzles
Assume one person is truthful and test for contradictions. Two statements that contradict each other mean exactly one is true, which is often the key. The number of truth-tellers given in the question limits your cases.
## Critical reasoning
Find the conclusion and the evidence. The gap between them is the assumption. To weaken, attack the gap: another cause, a biased sample or a bad comparison. To strengthen, close the gap. **Trap:** a true but irrelevant fact does nothing.
## Decision making
Number the conditions, and check each candidate against all of them. Note the exceptions. Missing information means "data inadequate", and you must not assume. "Over 60" and "at least 60" differ.
## Data sufficiency (reasoning)
Decide whether a single definite answer exists. A definite "no" counts as sufficient. Test each statement alone before combining them.
# Non-Verbal Reasoning
## Series (figures)
Follow one feature at a time: rotation, count, shading, position. Measure rotation as an angle per step. If nothing is steady, try alternate figures.
## Analogy (figures)
Describe the change in words, such as "turn 90° clockwise and shade". Apply both parts to the third figure. **Trap:** a mirror image and a rotation can look alike, so check an asymmetric mark.
## Odd one out (figures)
Check count of sides, lines and dots, then closed or open, symmetry, and whether three are rotations of one figure while the fourth is a mirror image.
## Mirror & water images
A vertical mirror swaps left and right. A water image swaps top and bottom. Mirror time = 11:60 − time. Water time for a clock with hour marks: 18:30 − time (subtract 12 when needed). Letters unchanged in a vertical mirror: A H I M O T U V W X Y.
## Paper folding & cutting
Unfold in reverse order, mirroring each cut across each fold. Each fold doubles the holes, unless a hole sits on the fold line. Then it forms one opened hole, not two.
## Embedded figures
Pick a distinctive feature of the simple figure, then find it in the larger one, at the same size and without turning. Eliminate options with lines the large figure doesn't contain.
## Pattern completion
Follow the lines that enter the gap, and use the symmetry of the design. Check for the same spacing and direction.
## Counting figures
Label the points, and count by size: single regions first, then pairs. Triangles with n lines from an apex to a base: n(n − 1)/2 for n lines. In an n × n grid: squares n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6, rectangles [n(n + 1)/2]².
## Cubes & dice
Painted cube of side n: 3 faces 8, 2 faces 12(n − 2), 1 face 6(n − 2)², none (n − 2)³. Standard dice: opposites add to 7. Faces seen together cannot be opposite.
## Figure matrix
Test rows, then columns. Look for count sums, each shape once per row, and rotation across a row. Confirm the rule on a complete row before answering.
# Grammar
## Parts of speech
A word's part of speech depends on its use. "Run" is a noun in "a long run", and a verb in "they run". Adverbs usually tell how, when or where, but "fast" and "hard" can be adjective or adverb without -ly.
## Tenses
| Tense | Signal | Form |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | every day, usually | writes |
| Present continuous | now, look! | is writing |
| Present perfect | already, just, since | has written |
| Simple past | yesterday, ago | wrote |
| Past perfect | before another past event | had written |
| Future perfect | by next year | will have written |

**Trap:** "know", "want" and "belong" rarely take the continuous form, and a clause with "if" or "when" about the future uses the present.
## Subject-verb agreement
Find the true subject: ignore phrases such as "along with" and "as well as". Each, every, either, neither take singular verbs. "A number of" is plural, and "the number of" is singular. With "or" and "nor", the nearer subject decides. Some nouns are always singular: news, information, furniture.
## Articles
A/an depends on the first **sound**: an hour, a university. The: unique things, superlatives, ordinals, rivers, oceans, mountain ranges and things already mentioned. No article: most languages, games, meals, and single mountains.
## Prepositions
At a point, on a surface, in an enclosed space. Time: at 5, on Monday, in June. For a length of time, since a starting point. Set phrases must be learnt: good at, interested in, depend on, afraid of, different from, married to.
## Pronouns
Subject form (I, he) before verbs. Object form (me, him) after prepositions: between you and me. Reflexives refer back to the subject. Who is a subject, and whom is an object. Possessives have no apostrophe: its, hers, theirs.
## Conjunctions
Pairs must match: both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also. Although takes a clause, and despite takes a noun: "Although it rained", "Despite the rain". Unless means if not, so don't add a negative.
## Active & passive voice
Passive = be + past participle, with the tense carried by "be". Object becomes subject. Verbs with no object have no passive. Drop the "by" phrase when the doer is unknown.
## Direct & indirect speech
Tense shifts back a step: is → was, did → had done, will → would. Pronouns change for the speaker. Time words change: today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there. Universal truths keep their tense. Questions lose their question order.
## Modifiers
Put a modifier next to what it describes. An opening phrase must describe the subject that follows the comma. "Only" and "nearly" change meaning with position.
## Parallelism
Items in a list, or joined by pairs, need the same form: reading, writing and swimming; not reading, to write and swims.
## Error spotting
Check in order: subject-verb agreement, tense, pronoun, preposition, article, then comparative forms. Common errors: "more better", "since two years", "discuss about", "informations", "did not went".
## Sentence correction
Eliminate options that keep the error, then compare what differs. Choose the shortest option that's grammatically complete and keeps the meaning. Watch out for redundancy: "return back", "repeat again".
# Vocabulary
## Synonyms & antonyms
Use roots: "benev-" is good, "mal-" is bad, "chron-" is time, "-phile" is lover. Try the word in a sentence, and check whether its tone is positive or negative. Choose the closest, not a merely related word.
## One-word substitution
Learn groups by ending: -cide (killing), -cracy (rule), -logy (study), -phobia (fear), -phile (lover). Patricide is the killing of one's father, and democracy is rule by the people.
## Idioms & phrases
Idioms can't be read literally, so learn them with a sentence. Group them by theme: effort, secrets, failure, or luck. If you do not know one, use the sentence's context for positive or negative tone.
## Spelling check
Rules help: i before e except after c (receive), with exceptions (weird, seize). Double the final consonant for a stressed short vowel: begin → beginning. Keep a list of your personal errors, and test on it often.
## Homonyms & confusables
Noun or verb is a frequent key: advice (noun), advise (verb); effect (usually noun), affect (usually verb); practice (noun), practise (verb, British). Others: principal (head) and principle (rule); stationary (still) and stationery (paper).
## Word analogy
State the relation in a sentence, then test each option in the same sentence. If several fit, make your sentence more exact.
## Foreign words & phrases
Learn the common ones: ad hoc, bona fide, de facto, per se, status quo, vice versa, faux pas, prima facie, modus operandi. Latin and French are the main sources in tests.
# Sentence Structure & Flow
## Fill in the blanks
Find the clue words: "although" reverses the meaning, "because" continues it. Predict the word before reading the options. With two blanks, eliminate using the easier one first.
## Cloze test
Read the whole passage first for its tone. Then check each blank's grammar: part of speech, tense, collocation. Reread at the end with your choices in place.
## Para jumbles
Find the opener: it introduces, and doesn't depend on another. Look for links: pronouns point back, "however" contrasts, and "then" orders. Find a mandatory pair, and use it to eliminate options.
## Odd sentence out
Name the paragraph's topic in a few words. The odd sentence may share a keyword while being about something else, so check the idea and not just the words.
## Sentence insertion
Read before and after the gap. The sentence must connect both: pronouns need a referent, and contrast words need a contrast.
# Comprehension
## Reading comprehension
Read the questions' stems first. Skim for structure, then read for the main idea, tone and the author's view. Answer from the passage, not outside knowledge. **Trap:** an option can be true yet not supported, or use extreme words.
## Paragraph summary
A summary covers the whole paragraph, with its main point and qualifications. Reject options that are too narrow, too broad, or add new ideas.
## Critical reasoning (verbal)
Name the flaw: correlation as cause, small sample, personal attack, false choice, slippery slope, appeal to authority or popularity, or circular reasoning. Then choose the option that targets it.
# Reading & Analysing Data
## Tables
Read headings and units first. Scan for what's asked before calculating. Approximate when options are far apart.
## Bar graphs
Read the scale and its starting point, as axes that don't start at zero exaggerate differences. Stacked bars show parts: subtract to find a segment.
## Line graphs
Steepness shows the rate of change. "Greatest increase" may mean absolute change or percentage, so read closely.
## Pie charts
1% = 3.6°. Value = share × total. Two pies with different totals can't be compared by share alone.
## Mixed or combined graphs
Identify which axis each series uses. Link figures through a shared item, such as a year.
## Radar or web charts
Each spoke is one variable, with the scale outward from the centre. Compare along a spoke, rather than by overall area.
## Caselets
Turn the paragraph into a table or Venn diagram before answering. Note percentage bases carefully, as "of the rest" changes the base.
## Venn-based data interpretation
Fill from the centre outwards: all three, then pairs, then singles, and then "none".
## Missing data
Set up an equation from a known total or average. Check the final answer against other given facts.
## Data sufficiency (data interpretation)
Don't calculate fully, as you only need to know whether a unique value exists. A percentage alone can't give an amount without a base.
## Core calculation skills
Use fractions for percentages: 12.5% = 1/8. Use approximations, and split: 18% = 10% + 8%. Compare a/b and c/d by cross-multiplying.
# Core Computer Science
## Computer fundamentals
Memory hierarchy, from fast and small to slow and large: registers, cache, RAM, SSD, disk. 2¹⁰ ≈ 10³. Two's complement of n bits covers −2ⁿ⁻¹ to 2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1. Know compiler, interpreter, linker and loader.
## Programming & output prediction
Trace by hand, using a table of variables. Watch integer division, ++ before and after, left-to-right string concatenation, short-circuit && and ||, array bounds, and static versus instance fields.
## Data structures & algorithms
| Operation | Array | Linked list | Hash table | Balanced BST |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Access by index or key | O(1) | O(n) | O(1) average | O(log n) |
| Insert at front | O(n) | O(1) | n/a | n/a |
| Search | O(n) | O(n) | O(1) average | O(log n) |

Sorts: merge O(n log n) and stable; quick O(n log n) on average, O(n²) worst; heap O(n log n), in place.
## OOP concepts
Four pillars: encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism, abstraction. Overriding is chosen at run time from the object. Overloading is chosen at compile time from the declared types. Fields and static methods use the declared type.
## DBMS & SQL
Clause order when run: FROM, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, SELECT, ORDER BY. COUNT(*) counts rows, and COUNT(col) skips NULLs. Normal forms: 1NF atomic, 2NF no partial dependency, 3NF no transitive dependency. Know ACID and isolation anomalies.
## Operating systems
Know FCFS, SJF, Round Robin. Waiting time = turnaround − burst. Four deadlock conditions: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, circular wait. Page replacement: FIFO, LRU, optimal.
## Computer networks
OSI: physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application. /n network: 2³²⁻ⁿ addresses, minus 2 usable hosts. Common ports: 22, 25, 53, 80, 443. TCP is reliable, and UDP is not.
## Logical & pseudocode questions
Trace with a small table. Watch the loop bounds, whether they include the end, and integer division. Recognise patterns: digit sum, reversal, GCD, primes, and counting.
